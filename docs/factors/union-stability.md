# Union stability at 30–44

```{warning}
This page was written by AI and has not been fully reviewed. Check figures and claims against the linked sources.
```

In England & Wales, 58% of the fall in synthetic TFR between 2010 and 2022 came from mothers having fewer children, while the share of women living in a couple at 30–44 rose by 3–4 points ([UK outcome grid](../uk/outcome-grid.md)). If couples at 30–44 split more often, or found new partners less often after a split, second and later births would fall even with coupling steady. This page checks that with three measures: the share currently divorced or separated, the share of previously married people living with a new partner, and lone mothers.

None of the public series counts cohabiting couples who split up. The ILO and ONS series use legal marital status in Europe and the US, so a never-married cohabiting couple that separates stays "never married"; each chart says which measure it uses.

## Findings

- **Fewer people at 30–44 are divorced or separated in the rich countries.** Among women aged 35–44 who have ever married, the divorced or separated share fell between 2010 and 2024 in the UK (22.0% to 13.7%), the US (19.6% to 16.8%), Canada (11.4% to 7.9%), Austria (15.4% to 10.7%) and Czechia (21.2% to 13.6%). Of 15 countries where ILO records legal status only, 10 fell between 2010 and 2019 (median −0.8 points). Of 24 other countries, mostly in Latin America, Asia and Africa, 20 rose (median +1.0 point).
- **In England & Wales the pool of separated people without a partner shrank.** Women aged 40–44 who were previously married and not in a couple fell from 15.6% of the age group in 2010 to 8.5% in 2025; at 35–39 from 9.3% to 4.9%. Fewer marry, and those who do divorce less.
- **Re-partnering after a marriage ends has fallen in England & Wales.** Of previously married people who have not remarried, the share cohabiting with a new partner fell at 40–44 from 26.6% to 20.3% for women and from 35.8% to 27.9% for men (2010 to 2025). Remarriages can't be separated from first marriages in this source, so these shares are lower bounds.
- **Lone motherhood rose in most of Europe.** Lone mothers as a share of women aged 25–49 living with children rose between 2010 and 2019 in 20 of 28 countries; the EU27 share went from 9.1% to 10.2%. The rise was largest in Estonia (14.3% to 20.8%) and Malta (5.0% to 9.6%); the UK fell from 22.0% to 19.8%.
- **Divorce rates fell.** The EU27 crude divorce rate was 1.9 per 1,000 people in 2010 and 1.6 in 2023, and it fell in 18 of 24 countries with both years.
- **Separation trends don't line up with second-birth trends.** Second births per woman with one child at 30–44 rose between 2010 and 2019 in 7 of 9 countries with ILO data and 12 of 16 with Eurostat data. Countries where separation fell most did not see bigger rises (r = −0.48, n = 9), and the change in lone motherhood is unrelated to it (r = 0.17, n = 16).

So the legal-marriage data point the other way from the hypothesis: fewer people at 30–44 are divorced or separated now than in 2010. What these sources can't rule out is more break-ups among cohabiting couples, who are a growing share of parents. The rise in lone mothers in most of Europe fits that, but it also counts mothers who never lived with a partner.
