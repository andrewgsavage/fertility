Fertility fell after 2010 in many rich countries at once, and rose in a few. A factor that explains the shared fall should have moved towards fewer births where fewer women were having children, and not where that held or rose. This page checks that for 36 countries between 2010 and 2019. Both years have TFR data for every country, and 2019 ends the window before COVID.

Columns are countries. The 33 with a 2010–2019 value for the share of women with a child by 30 come first, sorted by its change, with the largest fall on the left. For 10 of them the value is an estimate, marked ≈. Australia, France and New Zealand follow, sorted by TFR change, because their child-by-age rows compare other years. The 13 countries without HFD birth-order tables for 2010–2019 have no birth-rate cells for that window. TFR is shown above for context. Rows are the share of women with a child by age, birth rates by age, coupling by age, abortions per 100 births of the same order, and the factors with a cross-country series. Each cell shows how far that row moved in that country, coloured by direction: orange if it moved towards fewer births, aqua if the other way. A dot marks a cell whose start or end value comes from a different year. Hover a cell for the values. Click a country for its [profile](country-detail.md), or a factor for its page.

"Women in a couple" and "Couples with a child" show one summary row per 5-year age band. Each country uses its best coupling series for that band: a register first, then a census confirmed by another series, then EU-LFS 18–24 or ILO 15–24 for 20–24, then an unconfirmed census, faded. "Couples with a child" divides the share with a child by the end of the band (by 25 for 20–24, up to by 40 for 35–39) by that coupling share. Pick "All source rows" in a group's dropdown to see every series.

A factor that drives the shared fall would show orange on the left and neutral or aqua on the right. A factor that changed everywhere shows orange across the whole row. That fits the timing but not the differences between countries.

## What the matrix shows

**Fewer women had a child at every age to 40.** The median share fell 6.4 points by 25, 9.0 by 30, 6.5 by 35 and 3.9 by 40, so the gap at 40 is less than half the gap at 30. All 19 falling countries fell at each of the four ages.

**First births fell at young ages.** The share of women with a child by 30 fell in 32 of 36 countries; it rose in Czechia, Hungary, Romania and Slovakia. The median first-birth rate fell 33% at 20–24, 24% at 25–29 and 11% at 30–34, and rose 8% at 35–39.

**Second-birth rates were broadly constant.** The median changed by +9% at 20–24, +4% at 25–29, +4% at 30–34 and +16% at 35–39.

**Coupling fell at young ages.** The share of women in a couple fell in all 8 countries with data under 25 and 11 of 13 at 25–34 (median −3.9 points at 25–34, from +0.8 in Australia to −16.4 in South Korea, with Germany flat). Where narrower ages exist, the fall shrinks with age: −5.6 points at 25–29 (Denmark, Finland, the Netherlands), −2.9 at 30–34 and −1.8 at 35–39 (those three and the UK, where coupling rose at both ages). In the wider but lower-quality Eurostat series for 18–24, it fell in 8 of 9 falling countries and rose in Belgium, Bulgaria, Czechia, Germany, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia. In the 2011 and 2021 censuses, the 13 countries whose change another series confirms show a median change of −4.4 points at 20–24 and −3.9 at 25–34. All 9 of them with falling TFR fell at every age they report. South Korea fell furthest, by 16 points at 25–34. At 35–39 coupling barely moved: the median change was −0.7 points and 7 of 12 fell. Coupling was already falling before 2011: 8 of the 9 confirmed countries fell in the decade before at both ages, and Finland was flat. The European Social Survey points the same way but is too small to settle it: between about 2011 and 2017 the median change at 25–34 was −2.3 points in the 9 falling countries and +0.6 in the other 9, and only Norway's fall and the rises in Lithuania and Slovenia exceed survey noise.

**Fewer relationships led to a child in most countries.** The share of women with a child by 30, divided by the share in a couple at 25–34, fell in 7 of 9 countries. It rose in Spain and was flat in the UK. The fall is largest at young ages: the median change is −0.09 for a child by 20 against couples at 15–24, −0.08 by 27 against 25–29, −0.03 by 32 against 30–34 and about zero by 37 against 35–39. The census rows show the same with more countries: the ratio fell in all 13 confirmed countries at 25–34, median −0.13, and in 11 of 12 for a child by 25 against couples at 20–24, median −0.16, where Estonia rose. Child by 35 against couples at 35–39 fell in all 12, median −0.09, so at 35–39 fewer couples had a child while about as many women were in one. Before 2011 the ratio did not fall: the median at 25–34 was 0.00 across 8 confirmed countries. The ESS ratio fell in 16 of 18 countries, median −0.08, but most of its changes are within survey noise.

**First pregnancies were mostly kept, but less so at young ages where first births fell most.** Abortions to women with no children per 100 first births: at 25–29 the median change is +1.3 and at 30–34 −0.2, so a first pregnancy at the main first-birth ages was about as likely to be kept in 2019 as in 2010. At 20–24 the ratio rose in all 4 falling countries with data (Finland +14, Spain +13, Iceland +8, Italy +2) and fell in Czechia, Hungary and Slovakia, where the share with a child by 30 rose. Its change has a strong relation to the change in women with a child by 30 across 11 countries. Even so, fewer first pregnancies account for most of the fall in first births (see [Abortions and Births by Age](abortion-by-age.md#what-these-show)). Abortions to mothers per 100 later births fell at every age (median −8.0 at 20–24, −3.2 at 25–29, −2.8 at 30–34 and −7.5 at 35+); in the UK they rose at 35+ (+7.1).

**Women left home later** where the share with a child by 30 fell most (9 of 12 falling countries), but the median shift was about 0.1 years.

**Economic conditions improved.** Youth unemployment fell in 15 of the 19 falling countries, and young men not in work or study fell in 8 of 13. Neither can explain a fall in countries where they improved. Temporary contracts have a weak relation to the change in women with a child by 30.

**House prices rose more slowly after 2010.** The median price-to-income ratio rose about 14 index points over 2001–2010 and about 3 over 2010–2019, and its change has a weak relation to the change in women with a child by 30.

**Childcare expanded.** The share of under-3s in formal childcare rose in 13 of 14 falling countries with data, which should raise births.

**Mobile internet and degrees have the wrong sign.** Both rose in every falling country, but rose more where the share with a child by 30 fell least. They changed everywhere, so the timing fits, but they don't explain why some countries fell further than others.

## Limits

- "Falling countries" are the 19 where TFR fell by more than 0.1.
- With a child by 30 is estimated for Cyprus, Germany, Greece, Iceland, Italy, Latvia, Malta, Poland, Romania and Slovakia by summing first births per woman up to age 29. Tested on 17 countries with HFD tables, the Eurostat version of this estimate has a median gap of 1.2 points on the 2010–2019 change, but misses Bulgaria by 11 points.
- Australia's child-by-age rows use censuses for 2011 and 2021, so they span 10 years and include the start of COVID. New Zealand's use 2013 and 2018, so they span 5 years, and the 2018 question dropped the word "alive". Both measure real women rather than a synthetic cohort, with each exact age the mean of the two 5-year age groups either side. Both are left out of the cross-country correlations.
- France's Eurostat birth-order series breaks: first births go from 58% to 42% of births between 2006 and 2013, with 2007–2012 missing. Its child-by-age rows use 2013 and 2019 only and are left out of the cross-country correlations.
- Luxembourg is not included. Its birth-order series breaks in 2006.
- Dots mark values up to one year from 2010 or 2019, two for Australia, and three for New Zealand and France. Australia's ILO coupling series ends in 2017. Mobile internet starts in 2011, so its whole row is dotted.
- Relations between a factor and the change in women with a child by 30 are correlations across countries, for factors with 10 or more. They rank candidates; they don't show cause.
- Coupling at 25–29 covers 3 countries, and at 30–34 and 35–39 covers 4. All fell in TFR, so these rows show the pattern by age but can't contrast falling countries with the rest.
- Coupling under 25 uses 18–24 for registers and the US, and 15–24 for ILO countries. France, Germany, Poland, Spain and the UK at 25–34 use the FT's both-sexes series.
- The census coupling rows compare 2011 and 2021, except Japan and South Korea (2010 and 2020) and New Zealand (2013 and 2018), so every cell is dotted and most include COVID. The Before tab compares 2001 and 2011, 2005 and 2010 for Japan and South Korea, or 2006 and 2011 for Canada. Japan's 2020 census has more unknown marital status than 2010; excluding unknowns, as here, gives −1.1 at 25–34, and the official imputed figures give about −4.6. The UK's 25–34 cells are England and Wales; its 2011 and 2021 censuses publish 16–24 rather than 20–24, so its 2011–2021 cells in the 20–24 and 35–39 rows are 16–24 and 35–49 and left out of counts. The Before tab uses Eurostat's whole-UK 20–24 and 35–39 figures. Eurostat warns that census methods changed between rounds. The census and ESS ratio rows take the share with a child from the rows above, matched to within 2 years of each census or survey, and keep the coupling rows' faded cells and noise marks. Germany and Italy have no Before-tab ratio because their share with a child starts after 2003. Faded cells are not confirmed by another series and are left out of counts: 12 or 13 of 35 countries are confirmed for 2011–2021, and 9 of 27 or 28 for the earlier decade. Confirmed means within 3 points of the EU Labour Force Survey at 25–49, a national register, the FT or ILO, with no other series contradicting it. That survey has implausible moves of its own, and the check doesn't cover 20–24: Estonia's −19 points there is likely part artefact.
- The ESS row pools rounds 2–3 (about 2005), 5–6 (2011) and 8–9 (2017), so every cell is dotted. It covers countries with both rounds and at least 150 women aged 25–34 at each point. A typical country's change has a standard error of about 4.6 points, and ~ marks changes smaller than two standard errors. There is no ESS row for 20–24, where the standard error is about 6.6 points. The ESS partner variable changes between rounds 4 and 5, where the all-country share at 25–34 drops from 69% to 66%, so the 2005–2011 cells may include a break.
- The Eurostat 18–24 row counts couples living with parents as another household type. Denmark and Sweden are left out for series breaks, and values Eurostat flags as low reliability (`u`) are excluded.
- Central European countries (Czechia, Hungary, Bulgaria, Lithuania) were recovering from very late first births, so part of their rise is timing. The contrast with them overstates the relation for factors that also moved with that recovery.
- Abortion rows cover only the 12 countries that publish abortions by previous live births. England & Wales and Spain start in 2013, so their cells compare 2013 and 2019 and are dotted; DHSC publishes parity for 20–34 as one group, so England & Wales's 20–24, 25–29 and 30–34 cells all show its 20–34 value. They are indicative only: the value averages the three groups and hides any difference between them, so the cells are faded and left out of counts and fits. Its 35+ cells are DHSC's 35+. There are no all-ages abortion rows, because the all-ages ratio moves with the age mix of births. Iceland compares 2011 and 2018 and Germany 2010 and 2018 or 2020. Slovakia's reported induced abortions fell 26% in 2019 with no change in the law, so its cells may include a recording step.
- Desire rows are missing: no cross-country series covers 2010 and 2019 with the same question.
- UK birth rows are England & Wales, rebuilt from ONS cohort tables (see [UK: Which Outcomes Moved?](../uk/outcome-grid.md)), and start at age 21, so there is no UK rate at 20–24.
