# Coupling by employment status

```{warning}
This page was written by AI and has not been fully reviewed. Check figures and claims against the linked sources.
```

Part of [Left-behind young men](left-behind-young-men.md) and [Job insecurity](job-insecurity.md). Did the share of adults living with a partner fall mainly among men in weak economic positions, or across the board? Each section splits the couple share by employment status, then splits its change into two parts with a shift-share:

- **Employment mix**: the change that would have happened if each group's couple share had stayed fixed and only the size of the groups had changed.
- **Within groups**: the change in each group's couple share, weighted by the group's size.

The two parts add up to the total change. Both measures here count married and cohabiting couples together. Coupling over time, without the employment split, is on [Coupling over time](coupling-trends.md).

Changes in percentage points. Mix and within add to the change.

Men's couple share at 25–49 fell between 2010 and 2025 in 13 of 27 countries. In those 13 the change in employment mix added up to almost nothing: the within-group change was 105% of the summed fall, and the mix pushed coupling up in 7 of them, because the not-employed share fell (Spain 24.1% to 15.8%, Ireland 24.6% to 10.3%). So the fall did not come from more men being out of work.

Coupling fell in every employment group, but further among men without a job. In 11 of the 13 countries where men's coupling fell, not-employed men's couple share fell by more points than full-time men's. The gap between full-time and not-employed men widened in 25 of 27 countries, from a median of 21.9 points in 2010 to 27.2 in 2025. Because not-employed men are 6–19% of men in this age group, most of the fall in points still comes from employed men: in Spain, full-time men contribute −5.7 of the −8.1 within-group change.

Women look different. In 2010, women without a job were slightly more likely to live in a couple than full-time women (median gap −2.3 points), a group that includes mothers out of work; by 2025 they were less likely (+3.2). Women's coupling fell in 9 of 27 countries, and there too the mix contributed little (−2.8 of −34.9 points summed over the 9).

Where coupling rose (Poland, Czechia, Hungary, the Baltics, Sweden), the rise may partly reflect young couples moving out of shared homes into their own, which this household measure counts as new couples. Ages 25–49 mixes the age of first partnership with settled couples, so the fall at 25–34 is diluted.

The share of men aged 25–34 living with a partner fell from 50.0% in 2008 to 43.4% in 2018, then recovered to 46.0% in 2023. Over 2008–2023, 3.6 of the 4.0-point fall is within employment groups; the rise in men outside the labour force and not studying (5.8% to 7.8%) accounts for 0.4. Coupling fell in every group, by 3.3 points among full-time workers and 4.5–5.7 points among the part-time, unemployed and inactive. By earnings, the lowest quintile fell 4.0 points and the highest 3.4. Weakly placed men were far less often in a couple at every date (by 20–30 points against full-time workers), but the fall was across the board, and because 78% of men aged 25–34 work full-time, they carry most of it (−2.5 of −3.6). At 35–44 men's coupling barely changed.

Among women aged 25–34 the fall (−2.8) is mostly among those not in paid work and not studying (68.4% to 61.6%) and part-time workers (57.0% to 50.6%). Full-time women barely changed (53.1% to 52.6%). The lowest-earning women, mostly not working, were the most often coupled in 2008 (63.7%) and fell furthest (to 56.1%).

## What this shows

In Europe and the US, coupling fell across employment and earnings groups, not mainly among men in weak positions, and changes in the employment mix explain little of it (under half a point in the US; in 7 of the 13 European countries where men's coupling fell it pushed the other way, because employment rose). Men out of work or on low earnings were always much less often in a couple, and in Europe that gap widened. These are cross-sections: they cannot show whether a job makes a man more likely to find a partner or a partner makes him more likely to work. They also say nothing about potential partners' preferences; see [Left-behind young men](left-behind-young-men.md).

Not covered: ILOSTAT's labour-force-by-marital-status indicators (legal marriage only in Europe and the US) and EU-SILC microdata. The European table has no 25–34 band and does not separate unemployed from inactive men.
