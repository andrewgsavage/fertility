# Partner matching by education

```{warning}
This page was written by AI and has not been fully reviewed. Check figures and claims against the linked sources.
```

**Group:** Education and work. Related: [Left-behind young men](left-behind-young-men.md), [Coupling over time](coupling-trends.md), and the literature review on [educational assortative mating](../research/assortative-mating-and-fertility.md).

Two measures at ages 25–39: who is paired with whom in existing couples, and how many single men there are per single woman at each education level. If women hold most degrees and still prefer partners with at least their own education, single tertiary-educated women and single non-tertiary men should pile up on opposite sides of the market.

Terms: **hypogamy** = woman more educated than her partner; **hypergamy** = man more educated; **tertiary** = ISCED 5–8 (in the US, associate degree or more; in England and Wales, qualification level 4 or above). "Single" means not living with a spouse or cohabiting partner, so it includes people living with their parents.

| Woman aged 25–39 | 2008 | 2013 | 2018 | 2024 |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Woman more educated | 24.5% | 25.4% | 25.7% | 25.7% |
| Same level | 60.3% | 60.3% | 60.6% | 60.5% |
| Woman less educated | 15.2% | 14.3% | 13.7% | 13.8% |
| Mean age gap (man − woman, years) | 2.87 | 2.83 | 2.77 | 2.58 |
| Man 5+ years older | 27% | 27% | 25% | 23% |
| Woman older | 19% | 19% | 18% | 18% |

- Hypogamous couples outnumbered hypergamous ones in every year, by 1.6 to 1 in 2008 and 1.9 to 1 in 2024. Each share moved by about 1.5 points from 2008 to 2018 and was flat from 2018 to 2024; the shift from hypergamy to hypogamy described in the literature had mostly happened before 2008 for these ages.
- Cohabiting couples are more often hypogamous than married ones: 28.2% against 25.0% in 2024.
- The age gap is narrowing slowly. The median stayed at 2 years for all couples; for cohabiting couples it fell to 1 year in 2024.

| Single, aged 25–39 | 2008 | 2013 | 2018 | 2024 |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Non-tertiary men per tertiary woman | 1.88 | 1.69 | 1.55 | 1.35 |
| Men without a bachelor's per woman with one | 2.71 | 2.44 | 2.22 | 1.87 |
| Men below high school per tertiary woman | 0.42 | 0.34 | 0.25 | 0.20 |
| Tertiary men per tertiary woman | 0.91 | 0.88 | 0.93 | 0.92 |
| Non-tertiary men per non-tertiary woman | 1.19 | 1.22 | 1.26 | 1.34 |
| All single men per single woman | 1.08 | 1.08 | 1.11 | 1.13 |

- **The number of low-educated single men per tertiary single woman fell, not rose.** Non-tertiary single men per tertiary single woman went from 1.88 in 2008 to 1.35 in 2024, because single tertiary women grew from 4.2 million to 6.6 million while single non-tertiary men rose from 7.9 million to 9.2 million (2018) and then fell to 8.9 million.
- Among the tertiary-educated, single women outnumber single men in every year: 0.88–0.93 men per woman, with no trend.
- The surplus of single men is among the non-tertiary: 1.34 men per woman in 2024, up from 1.19 in 2008. That is a widening shortage of non-tertiary single women for non-tertiary single men, not a growing pool of low-educated men per graduate woman.

- Non-tertiary single men outnumber tertiary single women in 30 of 31 countries; only Ireland is below 1 (0.86). The highest ratios are Finland and Romania (2.69), Czechia (2.54) and Germany (2.50).
- Single tertiary men are fewer than single tertiary women in 25 of 31 countries (from 0.61 in Latvia); they outnumber them only in Switzerland (1.20), Germany (1.14), Luxembourg (1.12), Malta (1.08), the Netherlands (1.03) and Austria (1.01).
- Low-educated (ISCED 0–2) single men outnumber tertiary single women only in Iceland (1.30) and Malta (1.09). In most countries the excess single men have upper-secondary education, not less.
- Men aged 25–39 are single more often than women everywhere (median 1.35 single men per single woman; lowest England & Wales 1.04, highest Slovenia 1.55), consistent with men partnering later and with younger women.

## What this does and does not show

- The US is the only series with a trend. There, the ratio of low-educated single men to tertiary single women has fallen since 2008. The European figures are one census year, so whether that ratio has risen in Europe cannot be said from these tables.
- The ratios count stocks of singles, not who is looking for a partner, and they ignore age differences within 25–39, partners outside the age band, same-sex couples and migration.
- US couple shares concern couples that formed. They cannot show how many matches did not happen; see the counterfactual studies in the [literature review](../research/assortative-mating-and-fertility.md) (section 2).
