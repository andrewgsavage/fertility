If the fall in fertility after 2010 came from people wanting fewer children, mothers would also have been less likely to have a second child. This page compares, country by country, the change in the first-birth rate (births per woman with no children) with the change in the second-birth rate (births per mother of one) at the same ages. It is the [first vs second birth](../hfd/first-vs-second-birth.md) comparison, cut down from the [country comparison](country-comparison.md) to the rows that set first against later: birth rates, and abortions per 100 births of the same order.

## What it shows

**Mothers of one kept having second children while first births fell.** Across 2010–2019 the median first-birth rate fell 33% at 20–24, 22% at 25–29 and 11% at 30–34. The median second-birth rate rose at every age: +9%, +4% and +4%. At 20–24 the second-birth rate did better than the first-birth rate in all 24 countries with data, and at 25–29 in 24 of 25. The exception is Czechia, where first births rose.

**The gap is new.** In 2005–2010 first- and second-birth rates moved together at 30–34 and 35–39, and first births fell mainly at 20–24 (median −8%, against −1% at 25–29). The split between the two opened after 2010.

**Mothers became less likely to end a later pregnancy.** Abortions to mothers per 100 second or later births fell at every age: median −8.0 points at 20–24 (10 of 11 countries fell), −3.2 at 25–29, −2.8 at 30–34 and −7.5 at 35+. Abortions to women with no children per 100 first births barely moved at 25 and over, and rose at 20–24 in 7 of 11 countries (Finland +14, Spain +13, Portugal +10, Estonia +9, Iceland +8). So a young woman's first pregnancy became less likely to be kept, and a mother's later pregnancy more likely. Finland and Iceland are the exceptions, where abortions of later pregnancies rose at most ages.

**Second births fell in a few countries.** In South Korea (−29% at 25–29, −30% at 30–34) and Finland (−21%, −24%) the second-birth rate fell about half as much as the first-birth rate at 25–29 and as much at 30–34. Belgium, Denmark, Ireland, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain and Sweden had smaller falls at 30–34 (6–11%). In Chile the second-birth rate fell as much as the first-birth rate at 25–29 (−14% against −15%) and 30–34 (−9% against −8%), and fell 7% at 35–39, where first births rose 16%. In these countries the fall reaches beyond first births. Everywhere else, a woman who had one child was at least as likely to have a second as in 2010.

## Limits

- The second-birth rate is per mother of one at that age. Women who have their first child later have less time for a second, so later first births will lower completed second births even if this rate holds. See [Smaller Families?](../hfd/smaller-families.md).
- A rate that holds shows behaviour, not desire. Mothers of one having second children as often as before is consistent with the desire for a second child persisting, but doesn't measure it.
- Abortion rows cover 11 countries (12 at 35+), all in Europe and mostly central and southern; none of the non-European countries here has abortions by previous births.
