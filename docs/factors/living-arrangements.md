# Living arrangements

```{warning}
This page was written by AI and has not been fully reviewed. Check figures and claims against the linked sources.
```

The share of young adults living with a partner has fallen since 2010 (see [Coupling over time](coupling-trends.md)). This page asks where the uncoupled live instead: with their parents, alone, or with others (lone parents, flatmates, other relatives, institutions). Home-leaving ages are on [Leaving home](leaving-home.md).

The answer depends on the country. In Finland the fall in coupling was matched by more people living alone. In the Netherlands and the US it was matched mostly by more people living with their parents.

In Finland, men aged 25–34 living with a partner fell from 56.9% to 46.1% and those living alone rose from 25.9% to 34.5%; the share living with parents stayed near 8%. Women aged 18–24 living alone rose from 22.0% to 33.1%.

In the Netherlands, women aged 25–29 living with their parents rose from 9.0% to 16.0% and men from 21.2% to 27.4%, while living with a partner fell by 9.2 and 7.7 points. Women aged 18–24 living at home rose from 49.6% to 58.0%.

In the US, men aged 25–34 living with their parents rose from 15.1% (2010) to 18.8% (2023), up from 12.4% in 2000. Living alone moved by 1.5 points.

The "other" group barely moved anywhere: flatsharing and lone parenthood did not absorb the fall in coupling.

In the Netherlands the rise in living with parents peaks at 22–25: +13.5 points for women aged 23 and +8.6 for men aged 25. From 30 on it is under 3 points, about the same as the rise in living alone. In Finland living alone rose at every age from 18 to 39, by up to 14.7 points (women aged 21). Living with parents fell 4–6 points for men aged 18–22 and changed by less than 1.5 points from 25 on.

At 18–24 the fall in coupling in Western and Southern Europe and Türkiye came with a rise in living with parents of 4–7 points, larger than the coupling fall itself. In Central and Eastern Europe coupling at 18–24 did not fall and fewer young adults lived with their parents. Finland is the only Nordic country that passes the consistency check, and there the shift was to living alone, as in its register.

The rise in living with parents outweighs the coupling fall in most of the West and South, so it is not only the uncoupled who stayed home: young adults who would otherwise have lived alone or shared also stayed. Living with parents is a symptom here, not a measured cause; the same 18–24 data cannot say whether people stayed home because they had no partner or did not partner because they stayed home.
