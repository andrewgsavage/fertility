# Fewer couples or fewer births per couple

```{warning}
This page was written by AI and has not been fully reviewed. Check figures and claims against the linked sources.
```

Part of [Partnership formation](partnership-formation.md). The FT split the fall in the US TFR into fewer couples and fewer births per couple (ACS, 2000–2022: −14.5% from fewer couples, −2.5% from fewer births among couples; see [Coupling over time](coupling-trends.md)). This page makes the same split for every country with coupling rates by age and fertility rates for the same years.

**Method.** At each age, fertility is the share of women in a couple (married or cohabiting) times births per coupled woman, plus the share not in a couple times births per woman not in a couple. The change between two years splits exactly into:

- **fewer couples**: the change in the share in a couple, times the gap between births per coupled and per uncoupled woman;
- **births per coupled woman**: the change in births per coupled woman, times the share in a couple;
- **births per woman not in a couple**: the change in births per uncoupled woman, times the share not in a couple.

Shares and rates in each product are the means of the two years. Each part is summed over ages and shown as a % of the base-year TFR, as the FT did.

Only the US and England and Wales publish births split by whether the mother lives in a couple. Elsewhere every birth is counted as a couple's birth, so births per coupled woman is all births over coupled women. That overstates the "fewer couples" part: each lost couple is charged a couple's full birth rate, not the gap between coupled and uncoupled women. Running the US and England and Wales both ways shows how large the overstatement is.

- **US, 2008–2024:** TFR (ACS) fell 19.9%. Fewer couples account for 3.9 points, fewer births per coupled woman 3.4 and fewer births per uncoupled woman 12.6. Counting every birth as a couple's birth turns this into 10.1 from fewer couples and 9.9 from births per couple, so the shortcut more than doubles the couples part. The share of births to women in a couple rose from 69.4% to 76.6%.
- **England and Wales, 2010–2024:** TFR fell 26.6%: 2.5 points from fewer couples, 19.4 from births per coupled woman and 4.7 from births per uncoupled woman. With all births counted as couples' births: 3.7 and 22.9.
- **Shortcut countries, 2010 to latest:** fewer couples is the larger part in South Korea (39.1 of 38.8 points; births per coupled woman +0.3), Chile (19.0 of 25.4) and Japan (9.2 of 13.3). Births per coupled woman is the larger part in Finland (20.2 of 33.1), Canada (13.7 of 23.6) and Denmark (10.6 of 19.5); the Netherlands splits evenly (10.1 and 10.3 of 20.4). These couples parts are upper bounds.
- **UN census windows (2000s):** TFR rose in Bulgaria, Estonia and Ukraine while the couple share fell, so births per coupled woman rose (Bulgaria 2001–2011: −21.2 from fewer couples, +42.1 from births per couple).

In the US the fall is all under 35 (−25.7 points); ages 35 and over added 5.8. Births per coupled woman fell there (20–24: 0.20 to 0.13 a year) and rose at 35 and over, and births to uncoupled women fell at every age under 35 (20–24: 0.062 to 0.026). In England and Wales the share of women in a couple rose at 30–44 (35–39: 73.6% to 79.4%), so fewer couples count only under 30, while births per coupled woman fell at 15–29 (0.154 to 0.108) and 30–34 (0.142 to 0.112).

## Approximations

- **Couples are co-residential.** Births to women in a relationship who live apart count as births outside a couple. In England and Wales "same address" on a joint registration stands in for cohabitation, and married parents who live apart count as a couple. The ACS misses cohabiting couples where neither partner is the householder (for example a couple in her parents' home).
- **Period mismatch.** Couple status is measured at the survey or at registration, not at conception: a couple formed during the pregnancy counts as a couple, and one that split before the birth does not. LFS shares are for the year as a whole and registrations are by year of birth.
- **Shortcut countries.** Counting every birth as a couple's birth makes the "fewer couples" part an upper bound. In the US it more than doubled that part (3.9 to 10.1 points); in England and Wales it raised it from 2.5 to 3.7, because the couples share fell only under 30.
- **Coarse ages.** ILOSTAT gives 10-year groups and ONS one group under 30. A shift towards older ages within a group (fewer couples at 25, more at 33) is averaged away, and the split within a group depends on its age mix.
- **Different series.** ACS births and the E&W pooled groups give TFRs that differ from the official ones (US 2024: 1.75 against about 1.6). UN couple shares come from censuses and surveys that can differ by 10–20 points for the same country and year (see [Coupling over time](coupling-trends.md)).
