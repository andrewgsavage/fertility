# Education

```{warning}
This page was written by AI and has not been fully reviewed. Check figures and claims against the linked sources.
```

More years in education push first births later. Graduates have tended to have their first child after 30, so a rising share of graduates delays births across the population, and may reduce them if postponed births are not fully made up later.

The UK pages look at this in detail:

- [Education vs Fertility](../uk/education-vs-fertility.md): rising school-leaving ages and university entry for the 1968-75 cohorts opened a childlessness gap at 25-30 that had closed by 40; recent cohorts show a larger fall in early births than education alone explains.
- [Bye Bye Baby?](../uk/childlessness-by-education.md): first-birth rates fell by a similar amount for graduates and non-graduates since 2011, despite the sharper rise in non-graduate childlessness.

## Across countries, 2010–2019

The share of women aged 25–34 with a degree rose in every country; it rose more where the share of women with a child by 30 fell least.

## Has the fall been concentrated among less-educated women?

In rich countries, mostly not. Among women aged 25–44, birth rates fell at least as much for graduates as for women with less education in the US, Finland, Sweden, Denmark, Norway, Estonia, Poland and Turkey. Below 25, Nordic rates fell by 38–57% for women without a degree, and US rates fell most among women who had left school without one. Counting all ages from 18 to 44, US women out of school lost about a quarter of their births at every education level. In Korea, first births for women born in the early 1980s fell most among university graduates. In Mexico and Brazil since the mid-2010s, the fall at 25–44 was also largest among graduates. In lower-income countries surveyed by DHS, and in Uruguay, the fall was largest among the least educated.

Annual rates and completed family size give different answers. Graduates have their children later, so a delay shows up first as a fall in their annual rates. Completed family size by birth cohort is falling most among the least educated, because more of them stay childless. That is the finding for the Nordic countries (see [Nordic register studies](#nordic-register-studies) below).

### United States, 2008–2024

Counting ages 18–44 among women not in school, births per woman fell by 22–26% at every education level between 2008 and 2024. The age at which births fell differed. For women without a degree, rates fell before about 28 and rose after 31. For graduates, they fell at 22–34 and barely rose later.

At ages 25–44, graduates' birth rate fell from 1.71 to 1.42 births per woman between 2008 and 2024. The rate for women without a degree was about the same in 2024 as in 2008. In 2008 graduates had the highest rate. By 2024 they were level with women who had a high-school diploma or some college, and below women with no diploma.

### United States: birth certificates and children ever born

Birth certificates show the same pattern at 25–44, more sharply than the ACS. Between 2017 and 2024, graduates' births per woman fell from 1.37 to 1.21 (−12%). Women with a high-school diploma or less had slightly more births (+5–6%), and women with some college about the same. At 20–24, the birth rate fell by about a quarter for high-school graduates (122 to 92 per 1,000) and for women with some college, and by 11% for women with no diploma.

Children ever born shows family size so far. Between 2016 and 2024, childlessness at 30–39 rose by 8 points for high-school graduates (17% to 25%), 6 for some college, 5 for graduates (35% to 40%) and 3 for women with no diploma. Children per woman at 30–39 fell by 9–14% in every group. At 40–50 nothing has changed yet, since those women had most of their children before 2010.

### Europe, 2015 to the latest year

Of the seven countries where births per woman at 25–44 fell by 10% or more, tertiary-educated women had the largest fall in four: Turkey, Estonia, Sweden and Poland. In Norway the lowest and highest groups fell by about the same amount. In Romania and Finland the fall was among less-educated women, but both series have breaks. Single groups can jump between years, mostly because the coding of mothers' education on birth records changed (see the Nordic register rates below); countries marked † have such a jump.

### Estonia, register data

Statistics Estonia publishes births, women and children ever born by education from the same register. Between 2018 and 2025, births per woman at 25–44 fell by about 30% at every level: from 1.22 to 0.85 below upper secondary, 1.34 to 0.90 for upper secondary and 1.56 to 1.10 for tertiary. Most of the fall came after 2021. At 20–24 the rate fell 40% below upper secondary (98 to 59 per 1,000) and 48% for upper secondary.

Childlessness at 25–34 rose between 2012 and 2026 at every level, most for women with upper secondary education (28% to 46%), then below upper secondary (20% to 34%) and tertiary (42% to 54%). At 35–44 it barely moved.

### South Korea, cohort study

Korea has no published births by mother's education. A survey-based cohort study finds the opposite of the rich countries above: the fall in first births was concentrated among graduates. Compared with women born in 1960–64, the odds of a first birth for women born in 1980–84 were 39% lower for women with high school or less, 35% lower for two-year college and 66% lower for university graduates. For women born 1965–79 the education gap had narrowed. By 2018, when they were 34–38, 54% of women born 1980–84 had had a first birth, against 95% of those born 1960–64. Graduates are also a growing share: 45% of the 1980–84 cohort against 16% of the 1960–64 cohort.

## Uruguay, 2016–2021

Uruguay's TFR fell from 2.0 in 2015 to 1.4 in 2021, and the fall was largest among the least educated, the opposite of the postponement story above. [Cabella et al. (2024)](https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/50/16) report that during the decline the TFR of the least-educated women fell from 2.7 to 1.8, and that of the most educated from 1.6 to 1.2. The gap between them halved, from 1.1 to 0.6 children. They find that falls within each education group drove the decline, and that more women having more education added little to it. The fall in first births was likewise concentrated at the youngest ages (see [first births by age](../research/ft-birth-rates-everywhere.md#uruguay-first-births-by-age-20162021)).

Births to mothers with primary schooling or less fell 60% between 2013 and 2021, against 31% for secondary and a 6% rise for university. Mothers with a university education had 21% of first births in 2013 and 33% in 2021. These are counts of births, so they also reflect the shrinking number of women with only primary schooling. They don't measure each group's fertility.

| Mother's education | Births 2013 | Births 2021 | Change | Share of first births, 2013 | Share of first births, 2021 |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| Primary or less | 9,787 | 3,928 | −60% | 14% | 7% |
| Secondary | 28,025 | 19,446 | −31% | 64% | 60% |
| University | 8,004 | 8,500 | +6% | 21% | 33% |

```{note}
TFR by education: [Cabella et al. (2024)](https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/50/16), *Demographic Research* 50(16), citing Cabella and Velázquez (2023), an unpublished conference paper; the paper gives the figures for "the big decline" (2016–2021) without naming the years or the education groups' definitions. Births: Ministry of Public Health [Perinatal Information System (SIP)](https://uins.msp.gub.uy/) public microdata, 2013 and 2021, which covers 97% and 94% of births. Education is the mother's highest level of schooling recorded at the prenatal visit ("Estudios"); "none" is counted with primary. 1.4% of births in 2013 and 1.8% in 2021 have no education recorded and are left out.
```
