# Coupling and Partnership formation

Forming a partnership is generally a precsuor to having children, and this is seen in data - coupling rates correlate very well with fertility rates. 

Many argue that fewer relationships cause fewer children.

There's a few difficulties here:
- Data on people in relationships is less available than data on fertility rates. This makes it a less useful indicator for current trends - but may allow trends to be understood from previous years.
- Social norms have changed with time. In western countries especially, couples have kids outside marriage more than in the past, making marriage rates a less useful metric.
- Coupling rate data often aggregates ages, which makes trends that change with cohort take time to show up against noise in surveys. This is especially difficult when the data is aggregated into 10 year buckets, eg 25-34, as the recent fertility declines since the 2010s have been predominantly in younger women below 30, which makes looking at childbearing at end of term difficult.


- Studies and articles relating coupling rates and fertility rates often use TFR. TFR is difficult to reason around as it includes second and n-th births, which makes it sensitive to when couples form AND the desire for multiple children. My preference is to use metrics like % with child which does not suffer these issues, however this requires birth order data which is not always available.

Data and studies on marriage alone (age at first marriage, marriage decline in East Asia) are on the [Marriage](marriage.md) subpage, and coupling rates by year on [Coupling over time](coupling-trends.md). This page counts married and cohabiting couples together. The share of women with a child against coupling rates, by age, is on [Coupling and motherhood](coupling-vs-mothers.md). How long couples take to have a first child is on [Couple to first birth](union-to-first-birth.md).


## Literature

Studies that relate marriage, cohabitation or partnering to fertility, read from abstracts unless noted. They disagree on how much of the fall is fewer couples. In Europe, lower fertility within couples explains more of the recent fall than fewer couples; in East Asia, less marriage is now the main driver (see [Marriage](marriage.md)). The cross-country studies put the effect of fewer couples at about 0.1–0.3 children per woman.

### Men's earnings, marriage and births

Natural experiments on whether men's earnings move marriage and fertility together. They test the economic route into coupling, not coupling's effect on births.

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Autor, Dorn & Hanson 2019, *When work disappears*, American Economic Review: Insights 1(2): 161–178, [10.1257/aeri.20180010](https://doi.org/10.1257/aeri.20180010) | US local labour markets, China import competition, 1990–2014 | Trade shocks cut young men's employment and earnings relative to women's, and reduced marriage and fertility; the share of single mothers rose. |
| Kearney & Wilson 2018, *Male earnings, marriageable men, and nonmarital fertility*, Review of Economics and Statistics 100(4): 678–690, [10.1162/rest_a_00739](https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_00739) | US fracking boom | Wages and jobs for non-college men rose; marital and non-marital births rose, marriage did not. |
