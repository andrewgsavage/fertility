# Couple to first birth

```{warning}
This page was written by AI and has not been fully reviewed. Check figures and claims against the linked sources.
```

Part of [Partnership formation](partnership-formation.md). How long after moving in together (or marrying) couples have a first child, and whether that gap has grown.

**Why it matters for [Coupling and motherhood](coupling-vs-mothers.md).** Those charts plot % with a child against % in a couple over an age range. The *middle of age range* panels compare the two at the same ages, which assumes a first birth soon after coupling. The *end of age range* panels allow a lag of half the range: about 5 years for 25–34, 4 years for Eurostat's 18–24 (child by 25) and 2.5 years for the UN's 5-year groups.

## Findings

- **Median time to a first child.** Around 1990, for childless couples in Fertility and Family Survey (FFS) countries, it was about **4 years** in Northern and Western Europe and the US (5 in Finland and West Germany), **3** in Italy and Spain, and **2** in Central and Eastern Europe.
- **It has grown where there is a time series.** In Japan the mean time from moving in together to a first marital birth rose from 1.55 years in 1975 to 1.89 in 2000 and 2.45 in 2019. In S Korea, the mean time from starting married life to a first birth rose from 1.66 years in 2000 to 2.16 in 2018 and 2.5 in 2024, and the share of first births within 2 years fell from 77.5% to 52.6%. Fewer births conceived before marriage, which come soon after the couple moves in, account for some of the rise in Japan: these fell from 27.9% of marital first births in 2002 to 18.4% in 2019.
- **Share with a child after 3, 5 and 10 years (FFS, around 1990).** Northern and Western Europe and the US: 36–47% after 3 years, 54–68% after 5 and 75–89% after 10. Italy and Spain: about 60%, 79% and 91%. Central and Eastern Europe: 67–88%, 83–93% and 89–95%. No source found repeats this measure for later periods. That would need the GGS microdata.
- **Share of couples living with a child (censuses, 2010s).** Fell under 30 in New Zealand, Japan and South Korea. At 35–39 it rose in Japan (delay) and fell in South Korea by 7 points (fewer couples having a child at all).
- **US, the other way.** In the NSFG, the share of women with a pregnancy (leading to a live birth) in the first year of their first premarital cohabitation *rose*: 15% in 1995, 18% in 2002 and 19% in 2006–2010. Within 2 years it rose from 26% to 31%. This measures cohabitations before marriage, not all unions.
- **More first births happen with no marriage before them.** The share of first births within cohabitation rose from 12% or less in the 1970s to over half in Norway and France by 2000–04, and from 9% before 1985 to 27% in 2003–2010 in the US. Measured from marriage, the lag looks shorter than it is.
- **Age gap.** Around 1990, women's median age at first union was 1–2 years below their median age at first child in Central and Eastern Europe, 2–3 years below in Italy and Spain, and 3–6 years below in the north and west (6 in Sweden). Age at first marriage is no longer a guide: since 1990 it has risen past the age at first birth in most of Europe (Sweden −5.0 years in 2024, Spain −3.4 in 2023). Japan is the exception, where the two stay within a year of each other. Age at first cohabitation has barely moved: the gap from it to first birth widened from about 1.3 to 4.0 years in the US (1984–88 to 2006–10), from 3.7 to 5.6 in the UK (1980 to 2009) and from 3 to 4.5 years in France.

**For the coupling charts:** with lags of 2–5 years, the end-of-range panels are the fairer comparison almost everywhere outside Central and Eastern Europe around 1990, and more so since, because the lag has grown. The middle-of-range panels understate % with a child for a given coupling rate, and that understatement grows over time as the lag lengthens, which can look like a falling ratio. The lag is measured among couples who go on to have a child (register data) or censors union break-ups (FFS), so it is a lower bound on how long a coupled woman takes to become a mother.

## Couples living with children, by age

Of women living in a couple, the share who live with a child of their own fell after 2010 under age 30 in all three countries with census data at 5-year ages. Above 30 they differ. In Japan it held at 30–34 and rose at 35–39: couples had children later, as the longer time from moving in together to a first birth shows. In South Korea it also fell at 30–34 and 35–39, by 13 and 7 points, so fewer couples had a child at all, not only later. New Zealand fell slowly at 30–34 and 35–39, faster after 2018. In Japan the share barely moved between 2005 and 2010. Unlike the times above, these shares include couples who never have a child.

- **Europe.** In the 1970s, 1–12% of first births were within cohabitation in all six European countries with a time series. By 2000–04 the share was over half in Norway (54%) and France (52%), 31% in the UK and 27% in the Netherlands, but still 18% in Russia and 10% in Italy.
- **US.** 9% of women's first births before 1985 were within cohabitation, rising to 27% for first births in 2003–2010. Across all first births to women aged 15–49 in the 2015–2019 NSFG, 24% were within cohabitation and 53% within marriage ([NHSR 179](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr179.pdf), Table 7). In the Current Population Survey, 62–63% of first births in 2018–2020 and 2022–2024 were to married women and 16% to single women; the rest were to cohabiting women ([NCFMR FP-26-04](https://www.bgsu.edu/ncfmr/resources/data/family-profiles/womens-union-status-first-birth-fp-26-04.html)).
- **Marriage comes after moving in together.** 80% of US first marriages in 2020–2022 followed cohabitation, and women who cohabited first had a median age at marriage of 27 ([NCFMR FP-25-25](https://www.bgsu.edu/ncfmr/resources/data/family-profiles/FP-25-25.html)). For these couples the marriage date is a later point in a union that has already started.
- **UK: cohabitations lead to marriage less often.** Of first cohabitations begun in the 1980s, 53% had turned into marriage within 5 years and 19% had split up; for those begun in the 2000s it was 36% and 35% ([CPC Policy Briefing 50](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/439249/), Table 1, Understanding Society).

So where first births within cohabitation are common, marriage-based intervals cover a shrinking and more selected share of first births, and the marriage date comes later than the start of coupling. Measured from marriage, the lag looks shorter than it is, and age at first marriage overstates age at coupling. Where they are rare (Italy, and Japan and S Korea, where nearly all births are within marriage), marriage remains a reasonable stand-in for coupling.

- **US: cohabitation age flat, birth age rising.** Women's median age at first cohabitation was 22.8 for cohabitations begun in 1984–88 and 21.8 in 2006–10, while the mean age at first birth rose from 24.1 to 25.8. So the gap widened from about 1.3 to 4.0 years (2.2 to 3.6 from first union of either kind). Median age at first marriage rose from 22.9 to 25.9 over the same years, which is why age at marriage now tracks age at first birth, while the start of coupling does not.
- **UK: the same pattern.** Women's median age at first cohabitation was 21 for partnerships begun in 1980 and 22 in 2009, while the mean age at first birth (England and Wales) rose from 24.7 to 27.6. The gap widened from about 3.7 to 5.6 years. Women's median age at first marriage rose from 22 to 28, so by 2009 the average first birth came before the median first marriage.
- **France: the gap widened from 3 to 4.5 years.** Among people whose first cohabitation began before age 30, the gap between the median ages at first cohabitation and first birth was 3 years for unions begun in 1978–84 and 4.5 years twenty years later. Of those still together three years after moving in, 52% had a child for unions begun 1978–84, against 36% for 1999–2005. The median age at first cohabitation rose more slowly, from 22.6 for people born 1948–57 to 24.1 for those born 1968–77.
- **Finland: couples wait longer within the union.** Population registers show half of women born in the 1970s and 1980s had moved in with a partner by age 22–23, while the share who were mothers by 30 fell from 56% (born early 1970s) to 47% (born 1985–89). Couples in their first partnership delay a first birth more in each cohort, most of all those born in the 1990s ([Rahnu & Jalovaara 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcr.2023.100548), working paper Figures 2–3). The paper gives curves, not median ages, so Finland is not plotted.
- **Japan: no widening.** Mean age at marriage (counted from moving in where that came first) and mean age at first birth rose together, with the gap at about 1 year in both 1975 and 2019. This covers married couples only, which in Japan is nearly all parents.

So in the US, the UK and France the start of coupling has barely moved while first births have moved later, and the gap from moving in to a first birth has grown to about 4–5.5 years. That is longer than the marriage-to-birth gap implies, because marriage has moved later too.
