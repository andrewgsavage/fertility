# Outcome Grid

```{warning}
This page was written by AI and has not been fully reviewed. Check figures and claims against the linked sources.
```

Every factor in the [factors table](index.md) lowers fertility by acting on one of nine outcomes. A woman's path to a family has three stages: forming a couple, having a first child, and having a second or later child. A factor can act on each stage through one of three channels.

|  | Desire | Age | Ability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Couple** | Wanting a partner | When couples form | Finding a partner, if wanted |
| **First child** | Wanting children | Age at first birth | Having a first child, if wanted |
| **Second+ child** | Wanting more than one | Age at later births and spacing | Having more children, if wanted |

- **Desire** is measured by stated ideals and intentions in surveys.
- **Age** is measured by timing: age at partnership, mean age at each birth order, or the share who have reached a stage by a given age.
- **Ability** can't be measured directly. The realised outcome (ever partnered, ever a mother, mothers who go on to a second child) reflects desire and ability together. Where it falls by more than desire, the gap is ability: partners not found, infertility after delay, or costs that block intended births.

The split follows the motivation → intention → realisation chain used in fertility research. In Miller's traits–desires–intentions–behaviour model, motivations shape desires, desires become intentions about how many children to have and when, and intentions lead to behaviour ([Miller 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8045374/)). The desire column covers the first steps, and the age column covers intentions about timing. Ideal, intended and completed family size are measured separately: in 168 surveys from 37 European countries since 1979, the average ideal has stayed near two children while completed fertility fell below it ([Sobotka & Beaujouan 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1728-4457.2014.00691.x)). For women born in the early 1970s in 19 European countries and the US, completed fertility fell short of the intentions they stated at ages 20–24 in every country, and more women remained childless than had intended to ([Beaujouan & Berghammer 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11113-019-09516-3)). That shortfall is the ability column, with one caveat: not all of it is unmet need. In the British Household Panel Survey, people revise their expected family size in both directions as they age, change partner and have children, so part of the gap is changed minds rather than blocked intentions ([Iacovou & Tavares 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1728-4457.2011.00391.x)).

## Using the grid

Check which cells moved before asking which factors explain a fall. A factor only explains a decline if it acts on a cell that actually moved in that country and period. A factor that acts only on age explains the part of the delay that is never made up at older ages, not the delay itself. Whether couples at 30–44 split more often or re-partner less, which would act on the second+ child row, is checked on [Union stability](union-stability.md).

The cells need different data:

- **Couple**: living arrangements by age from labour force surveys and censuses. These count co-resident couples, not relationships.
- **First and second+ child**: births by order and mother's age, from the [Human Fertility Database](https://www.humanfertility.org) or national registers. Parity-specific rates (births to women at each parity, divided by women at that parity) separate a fall in first births from a fall in births to existing mothers.
- **Desire**: repeated surveys with the same question, such as the Generations and Gender Survey, Eurobarometer fertility modules and national family surveys. These are the sparsest series, and question wording and survey mode change between rounds.

## Further reading

Desire:

- Bachrach & Morgan (2013), [A cognitive–social model of fertility intentions](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1728-4457.2013.00612.x). Intentions stated young draw on family schemas more than on firm plans.
- Hartnett & Gemmill (2020), [Recent trends in U.S. childbearing intentions](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13524-020-00929-w). Whether the US fall after 2008 came from lower intentions or from intentions not realised.
- Kearney, Levine & Pardue (2022), [The puzzle of falling US birth rates since the Great Recession](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.36.1.151). Economic and policy factors don't account for the fall; shifting priorities might.
- Ajzen & Klobas (2013), [Fertility intentions: an approach based on the theory of planned behavior](https://doi.org/10.4054/demres.2013.29.8). Attitudes, norms and perceived control shape intentions.
- Billari, Philipov & Testa (2009), [Attitudes, norms and perceived behavioural control: explaining fertility intentions in Bulgaria](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10680-009-9187-9).
- Ní Bhrolcháin & Beaujouan (2011), [Uncertainty in fertility intentions in Britain, 1979–2007](https://doi.org/10.1553/populationyearbook2011s99).
- Gemmill (2019), [From some to none? Fertility expectation dynamics of permanently childless women](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13524-018-0739-7).
- Vignoli et al. (2020), [A reflection on economic uncertainty and fertility in Europe: the narrative framework](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41118-020-00094-3). Expectations about the future, not only current conditions, shape intentions.

Age:

- Hellstrand, Nisén & Myrskylä (2021), [Not just later, but fewer](https://doi.org/10.1215/00703370-9373618). Nordic cohorts are heading for smaller completed family size, not only later births.
- Leridon (2004), [Can assisted reproduction technology compensate for the natural decline in fertility with age?](https://doi.org/10.1093/humrep/deh304) How much delayed childbearing is recovered.
- Schmidt et al. (2012), [Demographic and medical consequences of the postponement of parenthood](https://doi.org/10.1093/humupd/dmr040).
- Ní Bhrolcháin & Beaujouan (2012), [Fertility postponement is largely due to rising educational enrolment](https://doi.org/10.1080/00324728.2012.697569).
- Beaujouan (2020), [Latest-late fertility? Decline and resurgence of late parenthood across the low-fertility countries](https://doi.org/10.1111/padr.12334).

Ability:

- Bongaarts (2001), [Fertility and reproductive preferences in post-transitional societies](https://doi.org/10.31899/pgy6.1015). Splits the gap between desired and actual family size into postponement, infecundity, competing preferences, unwanted births and other effects.
- Harknett & Hartnett (2014), [The gap between births intended and births achieved in 22 European countries](https://doi.org/10.1080/00324728.2014.899612).
- Berrington & Pattaro (2014), [Educational differences in fertility desires, intentions and behaviour](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcr.2013.12.003).
- Testa (2014), [On the positive correlation between education and fertility intentions in Europe](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcr.2014.01.005).
- Morgan & Rackin (2010), [The correspondence between fertility intentions and behavior in the United States](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1728-4457.2010.00319.x).
- Spéder & Kapitány (2014), [Failure to realize fertility intentions: a key aspect of the post-communist fertility transition](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11113-013-9313-6).

Couple and first child:

- Kreyenfeld & Konietzka (eds., 2017), [Childlessness in Europe](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-44667-7) (open access).
- Tanturri & Mencarini (2008), [Childless or childfree? Paths to voluntary childlessness in Italy](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1728-4457.2008.00205.x).
- Jalovaara et al. (2019), [Education, gender, and cohort fertility in the Nordic countries](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10680-018-9492-2).
