Synthetic cohort: a woman who lives through each year's first-, second- and later-birth rates for mothers at that parity, from age 20 to 45. It shows what that year's rates imply, separate from how many mothers there already are.

## What moved

**Coupling hardly moved.** The share of women aged 30–44 living with a partner rose by 3–4 points. Under 30 it fell by 1.4 points. Factors whose mechanism is fewer partnerships (smartphones, dating apps, the political divide, left-behind young men) predict a fall that is not in these data, at least for co-resident couples. They could still act through the quality or stability of couples, which this measure does not capture.

**First births moved most in timing.** The share of women who are mothers by 30 fell by 15.5 points. By 45 the fall is 5.4 points, so at 2022 rates about two-thirds of the delay is made up at later ages and a third is not. The age cell is the largest change in the grid.

**Desire for a first child fell.** Among childless 18–25-year-olds, the share saying they will definitely not have a child rose from 5–10% in 2005–07 to 15% in 2022–23. Over half said "definitely yes" in 2005–07. In 2022–23, just over half said "definitely" or "probably" yes ([Berrington, Kuang & Perelli-Harris 2024](https://www.cpc.ac.uk/res/docs/PB72_Intending_to__remain_childless_are_concerns_about_climate_change_and_overpopulation_the_cause.pdf), comparing the UK Generations and Gender Survey with [Ní Bhrolcháin et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1057/pt.2010.19)). The two surveys differ in mode and wording, so the size of the shift is uncertain; the direction is not.

**Mothers went on to fewer births.** The share of mothers who have a second child fell 4.1 points, and children per mother fell from 2.23 to 2.02. Splitting the fall in synthetic TFR (1.84 to 1.56) into its two parts, 42% comes from fewer women becoming mothers and 58% from fewer children per mother.

HFD's birth-order data for England & Wales starts in 2013 and shows the same direction: mean age at first birth 28.3 → 29.2 and at second birth 30.3 → 31.5 (2013–2022).

## What this means for the factors table

- The table's preset, "young first births, not second+", picks the factors that have their own pages. It leaves out the second+ cells, which account for over half the fall. Factors acting on mothers (childcare costs, home size, working hours, gender equality at home, intensive parenting) need pages too.
- Factors that act only through forming couples are not supported as drivers of the 2010s drop.
- The first-birth cells that moved are age and desire. A factor that only delays first births explains at most the third of the delay that is not made up later. A factor that lowers desire can explain the fall by 45 directly.
- No cell for ability can be isolated, because desire for coupling and for second births has no trend series. The Generations and Gender Survey's second wave and [Natsal-4](https://www.natsal.ac.uk/projects/natsal-4/) results, due in late 2026, could fill the desire cells.
