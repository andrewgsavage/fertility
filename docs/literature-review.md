# Literature Review

Academic papers relevant to this site, with a one-line summary of each paper's finding.

| Paper | Finding |
|---|---|
| Wilkoszewski, H. (2009), [*Age Trajectories of Social Policy Preferences: Support for Intergenerational Transfers from a Demographic Perspective*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1451738), paper prepared for the APSA Annual Meeting, Toronto. | Older respondents are less supportive of transfers to families but more supportive of pension reforms that shift costs onto younger generations; these age effects are non-linear across the life course and aren't fully explained by life-cycle economic theory alone. |
| Goerres, A., [*Reforming the Welfare State in Times of Grey Majorities: The Myth of an Opposition between Younger and Older Voters in Germany*](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1077604), published in *German Policy Studies*. | Finds little evidence of electoral antagonism between young and old in Germany: differences in party support across age groups stem from generational (cohort) effects tied to early political socialization rather than self-interested life-cycle voting, so age does not function as a political cleavage. |
| Buchmeier, Y. and Vogt, G. (2024), [*The Aging Democracy: Demographic Effects, Political Legitimacy, and the Quest for Generational Pluralism*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1537592723000981), *Perspectives on Politics* 22(1), pp.168-181. | Using Japan as a case study, identifies three effects of an ageing electorate on democracy: participation effects (younger voters become a numerical minority and turn out less), representation effects (younger people are underrepresented among MPs and candidates), and policy effects (a bias toward policies favouring the older majority); by 2060 an estimated four in five Japanese votes will come from voters aged 50+. |
| Aassve, A. (2024), [*Escape Is Hard, Once You Fall into the Demographic Trap*](https://www.unibocconi.it/en/news/escape-hard-once-you-fall-demographic-trap), Bocconi Knowledge. | Persistently low fertility creates a self-reinforcing "demographic trap": shrinking generations of potential parents produce ever-fewer births, making population decline increasingly hard for EU nations to reverse without policy interventions addressing social trust, economic security, and family support. |
| Morgan, S.P. and Taylor, M.G. (2006), [*Low Fertility at the Turn of the Twenty-First Century*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2849172/), *Annual Review of Sociology* 32, pp.375-399. | Nearly half the world's population now lives in countries at or below replacement-level fertility, marking a historic reversal from concerns over population explosion to concerns over population decline driven by sustained sub-replacement fertility. Table 1 gives "implied years to halve" (ln(2)/growth rate) for low-fertility countries at 2000-2005 rates, ranging from 41-46 years (Greece, Germany, Japan, Spain) to over 1,000 years (United States); cf. this site's own [half-life table](theory/demographic-half-life.md). |
| Berry, C. (2026), [*The Rise of Gerontocracy? Addressing the Intergenerational Democratic Deficit*](https://if.org.uk/research/the-rise-of-gerontocracy-addressing-the-intergenerational-democratic-deficit/), Intergenerational Foundation. | Argues Britain's ageing electorate is undermining democracy as older voters gain disproportionate influence at the ballot box; recommends lowering the voting age to 16 and giving young people political training and support to stand for elected office. |
| Sheng, D. and Montgomery, H.A. (2026), [*Generation Inequality in an Aging Society: The Demographic Welfare Dilemma*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/spol.70020), *Social Policy & Administration* 60(3), pp.551-571. | Japanese local governments prioritize elderly-focused policies to secure electoral support, potentially crowding out youth investment, a strategy that may ultimately undermine long-term economic productivity and future welfare outcomes for all generations. |
| Ahlfeldt, G.M., Maennig, W. and Mueller, S.Q. (2022), [*The Generation Gap in Direct Democracy: Age vs. Cohort Effects*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0176268021001026), *European Journal of Political Economy* 72, art. 102120. | Using 300+ Swiss referenda over four decades, finds a genuine ageing effect (not cohort or status-quo bias) on political attitudes: younger voters favour policies benefiting their own generation now or all generations in future (e.g. environmental protection), older voters are more conservative on constitutional and security questions; five referenda since 2004 would have had a different outcome under the 1981 age distribution. |
| Belschner, J. (2024), [*Too Young to Win? Exploring the Sources of Age-Related Electoral Disadvantage*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261379424000064), *Electoral Studies* 88, art. 102748. | Across 21 OECD countries, both young and senior candidates run for smaller, poorer, more marginal parties, spend less on campaigns, and are placed lower on party lists or win fewer preference votes than middle-aged candidates; young candidates are further disadvantaged by lacking political experience, with disadvantages following a non-linear, U-shaped pattern by age rather than a simple age gradient. |
| Chrisp, J. and Pearce, N. (2019), [*Grey Power: Towards a Political Economy of Older Voters in the UK*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-923X.12737), *The Political Quarterly* 90(4), pp.743-756. | Older UK voters have distinct material interests tied to housing wealth and pension income that show up directly in their political preferences, challenging the view that age-based voting divides are mainly cultural rather than driven by economic self-interest. |
| Vlandas, T., McArthur, D. and Ganslmeier, M. (2021), [*Ageing and the Economy: A Literature Review of Political and Policy Mechanisms*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2474736X.2021.1932532), *Political Research Exchange* 3(1), art. 1932532. | Systematic review of ~150 studies finds population ageing reshapes electoral behaviour, social policy preferences, and public spending; a larger 65+ population share is linked to lower inflation, more inflation-averse voters who punish incumbents for high inflation, and more economically orthodox party manifestos and central bank independence. |
| Vlandas, T. (2023), [*From Gerontocracy to Gerontonomia: The Politics of Economic Stagnation in Ageing Democracies*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1467-923X.13301), *The Political Quarterly* 94(3), pp.452-461. | Argues ageing electorates push governments to prioritise pensions and low inflation over social investment and growth, turning "gerontocracy" into "gerontonomia" - democratically-sustained economic stagnation - which breeds disillusionment and can push voters toward radical and extreme-right parties, threatening both democracy and capitalism. |
| Willetts, D. (2010), [*The Pinch: How the Baby Boomers Took Their Children's Future - And Why They Should Give It Back*](https://www.davidwilletts.co.uk/books). | Argues the baby boomer generation accumulated disproportionate wealth, housing, and pensions while leaving younger generations with worse economic prospects, and calls for a rebalancing of resources and opportunity between generations. |
| Willetts, D. (1992), [*Modern Conservatism*](https://www.davidwilletts.co.uk/books). | Traces the Conservative Party's political tradition from its Tory origins through Thatcherism, situating its approach to the state and society within that history. |
| Willetts, D. (2017), [*A University Education*](https://www.davidwilletts.co.uk/books). | Examines the expansion and funding of English higher education and students' paths to and through university, drawing on the author's role in introducing higher tuition fees. |
| Piano, C.E. (2025), [*Mind the Fertility Gap: Why People Stopped Having Babies and How Economic Freedom Can Help*](https://iea.org.uk/media/deregulate-to-boost-birth-rates-new-research-suggests/), Institute of Economic Affairs. | Argues that economic regulation, not just changing preferences, drives the gap between desired (~2.2) and actual (1.44, 2023) fertility in wealthy countries: states with greater labour-market freedom have smaller fertility gaps, land-use restrictions are linked to lower fertility (especially among women in their twenties), and even narrow rules like US car-seat mandates have measurably reduced births (an estimated 145,000 fewer since 1980). |
| Kulu, H., Kuang, B., Christison, S. and Berrington, A. (2025), [*Long-Term Fertility Trends by Birth Order in Britain: Comparison between England & Wales and Scotland*](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/epdf/10.1080/00324728.2025.2491354?needAccess=true), *Population Studies*. | Using census-linked administrative data over three decades, finds first-birth rates fell in the 1990s, rose slightly in the 2000s then fell again (driven by changes in timing), while second- and third-birth rates fell in the 1990s then stayed roughly stable (driven by changes in quantum, not spacing); trends are similar in England & Wales and Scotland but Scotland has notably lower second- and third-birth rates, a gap not explained by differences in education or ethnicity composition. |
| Washbrook, E., [*Do High Prices Deter Fertility? Evidence from England and Wales*](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/cmm/research/housing/publications/do-high-prices-deter-fertility.html), Centre for Multilevel Modelling, University of Bristol. | Using BHPS survey data (women aged 18-44, 1995-2008) matched to local-authority house prices, finds the effect of rising house prices on fertility depends on tenure: for home owners a 10% price rise raises fertility 2.4% in the following year (via a wealth effect, concentrated in second-and-higher births, but fading within two years so it shifts timing rather than completed family size), while renters - who get no wealth effect - show no positive response and are exposed to delayed negative effects. |
| Buh, B. (2026), [*Housing Costs, Tenure, and Fertility in the UK: How Rent and Mortgage Payments Relate to Childbirth*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10680-026-09778-6), *European Journal of Population*. | Using BHPS/Understanding Society data (1992-2021) and multilevel discrete-time hazard models, finds higher household housing expenditure is positively associated with first, second, and third births among owner-occupiers (reflecting investment in family-suited housing) but shows no clear relationship with births among private or social renters (where it more likely reflects financial pressure); this expenditure-fertility association holds across income quartiles and historical periods. |
| Barilari, F., Mastrorocco, N. and Paradisi, M. (2025), [*Population Aging, Voting, and Political Agendas*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0176268025001089), *European Journal of Political Economy* 90(B), art. 102730. | Using European Social Survey data, finds under-50s have become a minority of the voter population over two decades, driven by falling turnout among the young and rising turnout among the old, with each successive cohort voting less than the one before it throughout its life; manifesto analysis shows political parties shifting their agendas toward retirement and senior-focused policies, more strongly so in faster-ageing countries. |
| Li, W. (2024), [*Do Surging House Prices Discourage Fertility? Global Evidence, 1870-2012*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927537124000678), *Labour Economics* 90(C), art. 102572. | Using historical house-price data for a panel of countries from 1870-2012, treating housing and children as complementary goods, finds a 10% rise in real house prices is associated with 0.01-0.03 fewer births per woman - an effect comparable in size to that of rising female education over the same period. |
| van Wijk, D. and Feijten, P. (2025), [*Rising House Prices, Falling Fertility? How Rising House Prices Widen Fertility Differences between Tenure Groups*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12618740/), *European Journal of Population* 41(1), art. 33. | Using Dutch data (2012-2023), finds rising house prices lower overall fertility mainly by reducing homeownership among young adults and depressing fertility among renters in expensive markets, while owners who bought more than three years earlier see a positive home-equity effect on fertility; the net effect widens the fertility gap between housing-market insiders and outsiders. |
| Asili, S., Rezaei, S. and Najjar, L. (2014), [*Using Skew-Logistic Probability Density Function as a Model for Age-Specific Fertility Rate Pattern*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4055447/), *BioMed Research International* 2014, art. 790294. | Proposes the skew-logistic density as a parametric model of the age-specific fertility curve, arguing it fits unimodal patterns better than the skew-normal model of Mazzuco and Scarpa (2011), with smaller residuals and more precise parameter estimates. |
| Srivastava, U., Singh, K.K., Pandey, A. and Narayan, N. (2021), [*Experiments in Modeling Recent Indian Fertility Pattern*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-85959-z), *Scientific Reports* 11(1), art. 6592. | Models built on developed-country data (Peristera-Kostaki, Gompertz, skew-normal, Gamma-Pearson) fit India's unimodal, positively skewed single-year fertility curve poorly; four modified models fitted to NFHS-4 (2015-16) data beat both the originals and the Hadwiger model on AICc, with the modified Gompertz best for India overall, Uttar Pradesh and Gujarat. |
| Aliverti, E., Durante, D. and Scarpa, B. (2020), [*Projecting Proportionate Age-Specific Fertility Rates via Bayesian Skewed Processes*](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-42472-5_5), in *Developments in Demographic Forecasting*, Springer, pp.89-103. | Models the age-at-childbirth distribution as a skew-normal whose location and skewness evolve over time under Gaussian process priors, fitted by Monte Carlo inference; applied to Italian age-specific fertility rates for 1991-2014 with forecasts to 2030. |
| Whelton, T. and Spitzer, M. (2026), [*The Great State Pension Myth*](https://if.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/BRIEFING-NOTE_Great_state_pension_myth_FINAL_pdf.pdf), briefing note, Intergenerational Foundation (August). | Argues voters are not knowingly choosing intergenerational inequality but are misinformed: in an Opinium poll of 2,000 UK adults (July 2026), 37% wrongly believed their National Insurance is set aside for their own State Pension (36% correct), 43% wrongly thought pensioners are more likely than children or working-age adults to be in poverty (25% correct; after-housing-costs pensioner poverty has halved from 28% to 14% over 30 years), and only 38% knew pensioners receive over half of welfare spending (55%, £178bn, in 2025/26); Baby Boomers were more than twice as likely to get the poverty question wrong (45%) as right (22%). |
| Sorsa, T. (ed.) (2020), [*Population Policy Programme (Sustainable Population Development in Finland)*](https://www.vaestoliitto.fi/en/advocacy/sustainable-population-development/population-policy-programme/), Väestöliitto (Family Federation of Finland). | Sets out ten population-policy recommendations, including moderate population growth of about 0.3% a year to secure welfare-state finances, a total fertility rate target of 1.9, funding families so desired and actual family sizes converge, and substantially higher immigration (double the quota refugee intake) alongside reduced emigration. |
| Billari, F.C. and Kohler, H.-P. (2002), [*Patterns of Lowest-Low Fertility in Europe*](https://www.demogr.mpg.de/papers/working/wp-2002-040.pdf), MPIDR Working Paper WP-2002-040. | Across European countries, the median age at leaving the parental home for the 1960 birth cohort is clearly negatively associated with completed cohort fertility (Figure 6): every country with "latest-late" home-leaving also has lowest-low fertility, though not every lowest-low country leaves home late. Italy is latest (26.7 years for men, 23.6 for women) and Sweden earliest (20.2 and 18.6); the Southern European lowest-low pattern combines late home-leaving with low cohabitation and marriage-centred childbearing, while Central and Eastern Europe reaches lowest-low fertility despite earlier home-leaving. |
| Holdsworth, C. and Elliott, J. (2001), [*The Timing of Family Formation in Britain and Spain*](https://doi.org/10.5153/sro.597), *Sociological Research Online* 6(2), pp.54-70. | Using the British National Child Development Study and the 1991 Spanish Sociodemographic Survey, treats leaving home, partnership and first birth as a linked sequence rather than independent events; contrasts the Northern pattern (earlier home-leaving and marriage, weaker link between marriage and childbirth) with the Southern one (later marriage, but childbearing following soon after partnership). |
