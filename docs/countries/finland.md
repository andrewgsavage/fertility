Finland's total fertility rate (TFR) fell from 1.87 in 2010 to 1.35 in 2019, the lowest recorded in Finland or any Nordic country. It has been below the EU average since 2011.

## Väestöliitto population policy programme (2020)

Väestöliitto (the Family Federation of Finland) published its [Population Policy Programme](https://www.vaestoliitto.fi/en/advocacy/sustainable-population-development/population-policy-programme/) in autumn 2020, with a full report, [*Sustainable Population Development in Finland*](https://www.vaestoliitto.fi/en/?post_type=webpublication&p=16460) (Sorsa, ed.). It sets ten goals:

1. Population growth of about 0.3% a year.
2. Ecologically sustainable population development.
3. A TFR of 1.9.
4. Support for people to have the number of children they want: family planning, fertility advice and fertility treatment for all.
5. At least one close relationship for everyone.
6. Family-friendly work: flexible hours, remote work, zero tolerance of pregnancy discrimination.
7. Much higher immigration, including doubling the quota refugee intake.
8. Easier family reunification.
9. Regional policy that supports living and working in more than one place.
10. Less emigration, by recognising qualifications and experience gained abroad.

### Fertility chapter

The fertility chapter, by Anna Rotkirch, reports the following figures.

**Intentions and ideals**

- The ideal number of children averages slightly under two. 12-15% of adults aged 20-59 say zero is ideal.
- About 45% prefer two children and about 10% one. The share preferring three or more fell from about 40% to about 30%.
- Of Finns planning a child within two years, about 44% had one (Lainiala 2012). Across 22 European countries, 61% achieved an intention within three years (Harknett and Hartnett 2014).
- In the Helsinki region, 62% of men and 45% of women have no plans for children or are unsure.

**Childlessness and timing**

- 78% of 25-29-year-olds have no child; 87% of men that age in Uusimaa.
- Projected: more than one in five women and one in four men will stay childless.
- Mean age of first-time mothers was 29.6 in 2019.
- Childlessness is highest among the most and least educated women. Among men, more children go with higher education and income.
- Nearly 10% of women have four or more children, among the highest shares in Europe.

**Reasons given**

- Wanting to keep one's current lifestyle, the largest single reason for not having a first child.
- No partner, especially for men.
- Uncertainty about money, work, housing and childcare.
- Concern about career effects, especially for women.
- House prices in growth centres are more closely linked to birth rates than employment rates are.
- Fertility kept falling after the economy recovered from 2016, so the recession alone doesn't explain the fall.

**Policy proposals**

- Flexible working and part-time options, which Finns rank above higher benefits or new types of leave.
- More family-sized housing in growth centres, which Rotkirch suggests may be the most effective single measure.
- Adequate benefits for families considering a second or third child, and for low-income families.
- Fertility counselling, a review of fertility treatment and its legal barriers, and more reproductive health research.
- Support for student parents.
- Cash "baby bonuses" are described as mainly changing when people have children rather than how many.
