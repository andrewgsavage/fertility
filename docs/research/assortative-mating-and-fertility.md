# Educational assortative mating (homogamy, hypergamy, hypogamy) and fertility

```{warning}
This page was written by AI and has not been fully reviewed. Check figures and claims against the linked sources.
```

Scope: trends in couples' relative education; whether the reversed gender gap in education
reduces partnering; whether couple education type predicts first and later births; how much of
fertility decline couple composition explains. Europe, UK, US, East Asia. Researched 2026-11-19.

Terms (woman's perspective throughout): **homogamy** = same education level; **hypergamy** =
man more educated; **hypogamy** = woman more educated. Most studies use 3–4 broad levels, so
"homogamy" includes e.g. two upper-secondary partners. Shares depend heavily on the number of
categories, so compare trends within a study, not levels across studies.

## Bottom line

- **The flip from hypergamy to hypogamy is near-universal and tracks the gender education gap.**
  US: among couples with different education, the hypogamous share went from ~35% (marriages of
  the early 1950s) to >60% (2005–09). Europe (27 countries, ESS): from 1.5 hypergamous couples per
  hypogamous one (born before 1955) to 1.6 hypogamous per hypergamous (born 1980+); hypogamy
  outnumbers hypergamy in 23 of 27 countries. Germany, Austria, Switzerland and Slovakia are the
  laggards. Japan: hypogamy reached 20% of couples (wife 30–39) by 2010. Urban China (marriages
  2000–08): hypergamy 28%, hypogamy 14%. Homogamy stays the largest type everywhere.
- **Highly educated women in the West are not partnering less.** The losers are low-educated men
  and (US) non-college women. US cohorts 1930→1980: college women married at 45 fell
  77.7%→71.0%, non-college women 78.7%→52.4% (Chambers et al. 2026, working paper).
- **In Japan and Korea, marriage-market composition explains a minority of the marriage decline
  for university women** (Japan 1980→1995: ~15%; Korea 1985–89→2005–09: 8–17%), more for
  junior-college women (33%; 39–57%) and a lot for low-educated Korean men (14–37% for
  <high school; 73–86% for high school, by ages 40/45). Most of the decline is lower
  propensity to marry at given availability.
- **Fertility: the pattern is "pooling", not "who is more educated".** Couples where both are
  tertiary-educated start later but have the highest second and third birth rates (EU-SILC 13
  country groups; Finland; Sweden; US NLSY79). Couples with a low-educated partner have the
  lowest. Hypogamous vs hypergamous couples at the same education levels mostly don't differ
  (Finland register: not significant; Spain LFS: 1.9 children for both).
- **Hypogamy is penalised in more gender-traditional settings**: GGS odds of childlessness ×2.6
  (Austria) and ×1.5 (Bulgaria) for hypogamous vs medium-medium couples; not significant in France.
  Hypergamous couples (educated man, less educated woman) have among the *lowest* second/third
  birth rates in most EU-SILC countries, against Becker's specialisation model.
- **All couple-fertility evidence is descriptive.** None has a causal design for pairing → fertility.
  Most can't separate timing from quantum, and all condition on being in a union.
- **No study found that decomposes fertility decline into changes in couple education
  composition.** Since hypo- and hypergamous couples have similar fertility in most settings,
  the shift between them probably matters little. The plausible channel is the extensive margin:
  fewer unions among low-educated men and non-college women. The only causal evidence on that
  channel is about male earnings shocks (Autor, Dorn & Hanson 2019), not education ratios.

- IPUMS-International census microdata plus DHS, EU-LFS, EU-SILC, GGS and Korean census: 420

- In 2010, women aged 25–29 had higher college attainment than men in 139 countries (86% of world

- Esteve, García-Román & Permanyer 2012 (56 countries, IPUMS, age 25–34) is the predecessor.
  Described in Erát 2021 and Van Bavel et al. 2018.

### UK specifically

- Gap. No UK census- or UKHLS-based trend series in shares was found. The UK numbers here are
  the ESS cohort index above (small samples per cohort) and the Esteve global figures, which
  include UK samples but don't tabulate them.
- Chan & Halpin (ISER WP 2000-12, BHPS marital histories) model entry into hypo-, homo- and
  hypergamous first marriage. Their abstract says cohort, age and education participation have
  strong effects. **Abstract only.**

### Is the shift preference change or composition?

- Grow & Van Bavel 2015 (agent-based model, 12 European countries, cohorts 1921–2012, validated
  on ESS 2010/2012): the move from hypergamy to hypogamy is reproduced **with fixed preferences**
  (similar education, high earnings prospects, women prefer slightly older men). Composition
  drives the trend; preferences drive the level of homogamy. Removing the earnings-prospects
  preference raises simulated homogamy from 71% to 79%.
- Fukuda et al. (Japan) find hypogamy rising in log-linear odds too, net of marginals, so not
  composition alone. Much of the rise is junior-college wives with high-school husbands.
- Qian 2016 (US, cited in Van Bavel et al. 2018; not read): in most US couples where the wife has
  more education, the husband still earns more. Women stopped marrying up in education before they
  stopped marrying up in earnings.

## 2. Does the reversed gender gap reduce partnering?

### Marriage-market composition counterfactuals (descriptive accounting, not causal)

Method: hold availability ratios (unmarried men and women by age × education) at the earlier
period's values and recompute synthetic-cohort ever-married curves. Share of the observed decline
"due to" composition = 1 − counterfactual decline / observed decline.

- Korea: international marriage kept the share of men without high school married by 45 about
  10 pp higher than it would otherwise have been (Raymo & Park 2020).
- Japan: university women adjusted their pairing patterns less than junior-college women did.
- US cross-area (Chambers et al.): a 1 SD (7.6 pp) lower employment rate of non-college men goes
  with 4.2 pp lower marriage for non-college women and 2.4 pp for college women. This is a
  correlation.

Disagreement: Qian & Qian 2014 (urban CGSS 2000s, descriptive) find marriage rises with education
for men and falls for women, especially after 30, and attribute this to persistent hypergamy norms.
Huang et al.'s IV estimate goes the other way. Both may hold: education itself raises marriage
chances, while the gender-skewed pool at the top still leaves older tertiary women unmatched.

Simulation: Grow & Van Bavel 2015 reports couple types among the married. It does not report how
much non-marriage the reversal creates. No simulation of a pure singlehood effect was found.

Van Bavel et al. 2018 review: in the US and Canada the education gradient in women's marriage
reversed (college women now as likely or more likely to marry). In Europe by 2010 it had reversed
in the UK and Ireland, was positive in Sweden and Finland before 1995, and stayed negative in
Southern Europe. The negative gradient is linked to agreement with "men have more right to a job"
(Bertrand et al. 2016, cited there).

## 4. How much of the decline does couple composition explain?

- **Not found.** No study decomposes period or cohort fertility decline into changes in couple
  education composition vs changes in pairing-specific fertility.
- Closest pieces:
  - Andersson et al. 2026 decompose Finnish cohort fertility *levels* by couple pairing (one set
    of cohorts, no change over time). Abstract only.
  - Raymo & Iwasawa (Japan) and Raymo & Park (Korea) decompose the *marriage* decline (table in
    section 2). Because almost all births in Japan and Korea are marital, these are the nearest
    estimates of a pairing-composition effect on fertility, but neither converts them to births.
  - Chambers et al. 2026 (US): without the 10 pp of adaptation (college women marrying non-college
    men), college women's marriage at 45 would have fallen 16.7 pp instead of 6.7 pp.
  - Autor, Dorn & Hanson 2019: causal effect of male earnings shocks on births. This is the
    marriageable-men channel, not education ratios per se.
- Inference (mine, not from a paper): in Finland and Spain, hypo- and hypergamous couples at
  similar education levels have about the same fertility. So the hyper→hypo shift itself is
  unlikely to account for much decline there. Any composition effect runs through who stays
  unpartnered (low-educated men; non-college women in the US) and through more both-tertiary
  couples, who have *higher* parity progression but later starts. In Austria and Bulgaria, where
  hypogamy carries a childlessness penalty, the shift could plausibly lower fertility, but nobody
  has quantified it.
