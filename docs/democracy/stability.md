# Civilisation Stability

Population collapse is a problem that does not register in public consciousness until it is unrecoverable.

A fertility rate of 2.1 is stable. A fertility rate of 1.05 means the population reduces by half each generation, roughly 30 years - the mean age of childbirth.

```{note}
Half-life = 30 × ln(0.5) / ln(TFR / 2.1), where 30 years is the mean age of childbirth and 2.1 is the replacement TFR. Each generation multiplies the population by TFR / 2.1, so this is just the number of generations to halve, converted to years. Computed for TFR = 0.50 to 2.09 in steps of 0.01, including the chart and the Data tab's table; see `democracy/scripts/half_life_tfr.py`.
```

When the half-life is greater than a lifetime it seems a far away issue. However, this is precarious. Small reductions in the fertility rate, as has been seen in almost all developed countries globally, lead to half-lives dropping at an exponential rate.

## The point of no return

When fertility drops well below replacement <1.4 it is arguably impossible for it to recover; no country has dropped to this level and returned to replacement level.

It's worth pausing for a moment here. Returning to replacement level does not undo the reduction in births, it means the number of births is stable at a number fewer than previous generations. To 'undo' a fertility rate of 1.4 for a generation and return to the number of births in previous generations requires a fertility rate of 3 - which is unthinkable in a developed nation outside Israel.

When a population goes quickly from near replacement fertility to well below replacement fertility, this leaves subsequent generations with a high dependency ratio. There's a boon for the first 20 years as there's fewer children to teach, then for the next 45+ years there's fewer workers to support the elderly. This big time lag between a baby being born and retiring is a major problem.

## The cost of an ageing population

The high dependency ratio means current generations will have to support more elderly than any previous generation, so are likely to see lower living standards than previous generations. This flies in the face of social expectations, where people expect future generations to have living standards that are as good or better than their own.

The UK's government projections show this clearly with spending on elderly (Health & Pension) increase in the order of 50% in the next 45 years, leading to overall taxes increasing from 40% to 50% for the current level of spending.

## Democracy's demographic feedback loop

As the voter base becomes increasingly old, it becomes increasingly difficult to enact policies that lead to long term growth. People vote for their own interests, not the interests of their country's long term future. Elderly tend to vote for policies that favour current spending over long term growth.

Politicians create policy to win votes and have a stable economy in the short term. Fertility plays out over the long term, much longer than a politician's tenure, so is very difficult to prioritise - consider that it takes 20 years for a child born today to become productive; a child does not have a payback within a retiree's lifetime so does not benefit them. The policies required to increase fertility generally cost money. Furthermore, providing benefits that the elderly did not receive themselves is a difficult sell.

This is a disastrous feedback loop. The population ages, requiring more spending on pensions and healthcare for the same living standards as previous generations. Fewer workers must burden these costs, so these generations have lower standards. It becomes increasingly difficult to shift spending towards fertility policies due to the large elderly population who will see no benefit from such policies, and may see disbenefit through lower current day spending.

Buchmeier & Vogt identify three ways this plays out in an aging democracy: **participation effects** (younger voters become a numerical minority and turn out less), **representation effects** (younger people are underrepresented among MPs and candidates), and **policy effects** (a bias toward policies, such as social and fiscal policy, that favour the older majority). Using Japan - the country with the oldest electorate in the world - as a case study, they estimate that by 2060 nearly four in five votes will come from voters aged 50 or older.

```{note}
Y Buchmeier and G Vogt, ["The Aging Democracy: Demographic Effects, Political Legitimacy, and the Quest for Generational Pluralism"](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1537592723000981), *Perspectives on Politics* 22(1), March 2024, pp.168-181.
```
