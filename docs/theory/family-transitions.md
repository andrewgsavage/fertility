- **Single → couple has mostly fallen.** In most countries a smaller share of young adults live with a partner, especially under 25. Rises are few and small, mainly among women in their 30s and early 40s. See [Coupling over time](../factors/coupling-trends.md).
- **Couple → first birth has fallen at young ages and is flat by the late 30s.** Mothers per woman in a couple by age 30 fell in 9 of 12 countries, held in Spain and the UK and rose in Chile. In Denmark, Finland and the Netherlands the ratio fell most under 30 and was back at its 2009–11 level by 35–39. See [Coupling and motherhood](../factors/coupling-vs-mothers.md).
- **First → subsequent births has stayed broadly static.** Second-birth rates among mothers held or rose in 21 of 26 countries, while first-birth rates fell in 24. The clearest falls in second-birth rates are in South Korea, Finland, Chile and Sweden. Second births still fell overall, largely because fewer women become mothers. See [First vs Second Birth](../hfd/first-vs-second-birth.md).

## Critique

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single → couple has fallen | Holds at every age in the registers | 0.69 at 18–24, rising to 0.94 at 35–39 | A share of all women, not a rate; flat or up at 30–44 in England and Wales |
| Couple → first birth varies | Varies mainly by age | 0.66 at 18–24, rising to 1.01 at 35–39 | A ratio of two stocks, age-matched in three countries only |
| First → subsequent is static | Holds in most countries | Second-birth rate 1.02 against first-birth rate 0.75 | Rates do not control for time since first birth |

### The links are not measured the same way

The strips above use three kinds of measure: a share of all women (single → couple), a ratio of two shares (couple → first birth) and a conditional rate (childless → first, first → second). Only the conditional rates are transitions, so the size of a change cannot be compared between strips. A value of 0.69 for coupling and 0.52 for first births does not mean first births fell further than coupling.

Comparable measures would be entries into a couple per single woman and first births per childless woman in a couple, by age. Both need linked registers.

### How much of the fall in mothers is fewer couples?

The share of women with a child equals the share in a couple times the ratio, so the two can be separated.

| Age | % with a child | % in a couple | Mothers per coupled woman | Share of fall from the ratio |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| 18–24 | 0.48 | 0.71 | 0.66 | 46–65% |
| 25–29 | 0.68 | 0.90 | 0.76 | 63–73% |
| 30–34 | 0.87 | 0.94 | 0.92 | 40–61% |
| 35–39 | 0.95 | 0.95 | 1.01 | – |

Below 35, fewer couples explain roughly a third to a half of the fall in mothers, and the lower ratio the rest. At 25–29, where the fall in percentage points is largest, the ratio accounts for about two-thirds. [Hellstrand, Nisén & Myrskylä (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10680-022-09605-8) reach a similar result for Finland with individual histories: fertility within unions explains about three-quarters of the first-birth decline since 2010.

Censuses give the same split for more countries. In the 13 whose census coupling another series confirms, the 2010s fall in mothers was mostly within couples except at 20–24:

Coupling was already falling before 2011, at a median −3.8 points at 25–34 between 2001 and 2011, while mothers per coupled woman held steady (median 0.00). Fewer couples alone did not bring the fall in mothers; what changed after 2010 was the ratio.

### Single → couple

The share in a couple can fall because people couple later, couple less or separate more. In Finland, fewer marriages explain 19% of the first-birth decline since 2010, fewer cohabitations 2–4% and more dissolution 6% (Hellstrand et al.).

The fall is not universal. In England and Wales the share of women in a couple at 30–44 is flat or slightly up ([Coupling over time](../factors/coupling-trends.md)). In Sweden first-marriage formation fell from the early 2010s but cohabitation formation did not ([Cantalini, Ohlsson-Wijk & Andersson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10680-024-09703-9)).

### Couple → first birth

The charts on [Coupling and motherhood](../factors/coupling-vs-mothers.md) compare motherhood by 30 and by 35 with coupling at 25–34. On that basis the ratio by 30 fell in 9 of 12 countries, while by 35 it rose in 6 and fell in 4. The rises by 35 are largely an artefact: when coupling moves later, the 25–34 share falls while women can still become mothers by 35. South Korea's ratio fell by 0.32 by 30 and rose by 0.28 by 35. With ages matched, no age band in the three register countries rises by more than 0.05 relative to 2009–11.

The ratio has three limits:

- **It divides a cumulative count by a current one.** Mothers stay mothers after separating but leave the couple count, so rising separation raises the ratio with no change in births.
- **It has no lag.** A woman who coupled at 29 has had less time to have a child by 30 than one who coupled at 25, so later coupling lowers the ratio at a fixed age even if every couple eventually has a child. Part of the fall at young ages may therefore be a coupling effect counted as a within-couple effect.
- **It does not link individuals.** The mothers in the numerator need not be the women in the denominator.

- **Exceptions in both directions.** Second-birth rates fell clearly in South Korea, Finland, Chile and Sweden. They rose by about 20% or more in Russia, Hungary, Belarus, Bulgaria, Lithuania, Slovenia and Portugal.
- **No control for time since first birth.** Second births are most likely soon after a first. With later first births, mothers at a given age are closer to their first birth, which raises the rate at that age with no change in behaviour. The largest rise is at 35–39 (median 1.16). HFD has no duration-specific rates.
- **Mothers are a changing group.** If the women who no longer become mothers were less likely to have a second child, the rate among remaining mothers rises by itself.
- **Fewer years.** A constant rate at each age still means fewer second births per mother if the first comes later ([Smaller Families?](../hfd/smaller-families.md)).

Static rates do not mean second births held up. First births are 44% of the summed fall in TFR across the 21 countries where it fell by more than 0.05, second births 35% and third and later births 21%. With second-birth rates steady, much of that fall follows from fewer women becoming mothers.


Older cohorts show a different pattern. [Geruso & Spears (2025)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33913) find childlessness explains 38% of the cohort fertility decline in advanced economies. [Zeman et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.4054/DemRes.2018.38.25) find that for women born 1955–1970 falling second births drove the decline in Central and Eastern Europe, and first births in German-speaking countries, Southern Europe and East Asia.

### Delayed or forgone?

The falls shrink with age, which is what delay would produce. The share of women with a child fell by 9.5–12.9 points at 25–29 and by 0–5.1 points at 35–39. This does not show that the births will be recovered:

- Women aged 35–39 in the latest years were 25–29 around 2013, early in the fall. The cohorts with the largest falls at young ages have not reached 35.
- First-birth rates at 35–39 are at their 2009–11 level (1.03), not above it. A larger childless pool at the same rate does produce more first births, but the rate shows no extra catch-up.
- The share with a child at 35–39 is already lower in Denmark (82% to 78%) and Finland (75% to 70%).

Following each birth cohort as it reaches 35–39 would settle this for the cohorts now in their early thirties.

Censuses that split couples by whether they live with children point both ways ([Couple to first birth](#couples-living-with-children-by-age)). In Japan the share of women in a couple living with a child fell under 30 between 2010 and 2020 but held at 30–34 and rose at 35–39, which fits delay. In South Korea it fell at every age, by 13 points at 30–34, which fits births forgone.

### The chain is not a single path

Couples separate and repartner, and some women have a first birth without a co-resident partner. In South Africa and Namibia at least 45% of women aged 25–29 are mothers outside a union ([Coupling and motherhood](../factors/coupling-vs-mothers.md)), so the chain describes low-fertility countries better than high-fertility ones.

### Accounting is not causation

The chain shows where the fall appears, not why it happened.

- **Reverse causation.** People who want children may seek or keep a partner, so falling desire for children could lower coupling. [Chiocchio & Garstenauer (2026)](https://fchiocchio.github.io/files/Papers/FewerCouplesFewerBirths.pdf) state that both directions are plausible and do not separate them. Against a purely desire-led story, "no suitable partner" is the most common reason given for unmet fertility intentions in 12 European countries ([Sturm, Koops & Rutigliano 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10680-023-09664-5)).
- **Selection into couples.** If the people who stop coupling are those least likely to have children, births per couple rise with no change in behaviour, so a flat ratio could hide a real decline.
- **Changing meaning of a couple.** Cohabitation has spread, and across 15 countries cohabiting mothers have lower second-conception risks than married mothers, a gap that narrows but does not close ([Perelli-Harris 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10680-014-9320-2)). A constant share in a couple can hide a shift towards unions less likely to have children.

### Coverage

The age-matched coupling evidence comes from Denmark, Finland and the Netherlands, where cohabitation is common. It may not carry over to East Asia, where falling marriage is the main recent driver and South Korea's second-birth rate has also fallen. Published decompositions differ by setting, and most measure all births per couple, so a fall in second births would also count as lower couple fertility.

## What the chain supports

**Since 2010, fewer women have become mothers at young ages, through both fewer couples and fewer mothers per coupled woman, with the second the larger part at 25–29. Second-birth rates among mothers have broadly held, so second births have fallen largely because fewer women become mothers.** The data do not yet show whether the missing first births are delayed or forgone, and they do not establish which link is the cause.
